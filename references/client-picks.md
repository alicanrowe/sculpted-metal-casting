# Client picks: proven moves and flags

Every client review leaves two kinds of notes: details they love, and details they flag. Keep both in a file like this one for each collection, and read it before every new piece. Reuse the loved details when a new piece needs a seat, a centrepiece or a hanging detail, and don't "fix" them away in a check pass. Turn every flag into a rule.

Below are the notes from one review of a nine-way sawhorse comparison sheet (Nov 2026), written as general moves. The variant names refer to the influence-led set in `influence-weighting.md`.

## Loved
1. **Wrapped-cloth seat (WASH).** A leather pad built like a Korean wrapping cloth (bojagi), not a tube with straps: a superellipse pad with a soft crowned top and a flat underside, patchwork panels split by slightly different slanted seams in black, washed charcoal and washed indigo (no two neighbours the same tone), each end gathered into a knot with two flat cloth ears running out to soft points, deliberately asymmetric. A glossier leather tether runs from each knot, loops the rail neck and closes on a cast toggle. Slip the toggles and the seat lifts off. No buckles. Leather look: fine grain bump plus an uneven washed-dye multiply.
2. **Petal-hem saddle (THREAD).** A washed indigo-grey bolster, fuller on top and flatter below, with a channel underneath that drops over the bar (it locates, it doesn't clamp). Scalloped petal flaps of slightly different lengths hang from both flanks. Two thin drawstring cords tie at the front with tails ending in metal aglets drawn to needles. Matte with sheen, soft dye clouds. Pairs with lightly oxidised metal.
3. **Carved-feather stretcher (CARVED).** Moth wings made as fans of individually placed carved feathers from one root, the longest feather landing on the leg as a brace, with a solid web where it meets the leg so it casts as one. Carved on both faces. A claw-set centre stone at the thorax and small clusters where feathers converge. (Cap that centre stone to a buyable size: see `stone-sourcing.md`.)
4. **Unequal chain drops.** Two fine chains of unequal length hang from one bead, each ending in a claw-set pear of a different colour. A hidden core wire threads the links so chain and pear cast as one with the body. The same pair works on pendants, ear pieces and clips.
5. **Classic bolster.** Matte black leather bolster, piped ends, two glossier straps with liquid metal buckles.
6. **Fine-wire moth body (THREAD).** A light body and lower wings drawn in fine washed wire with pale stones: the counterweight to the heavy carved versions.
7. **Thorned vines on the posts (THORN).** A thorned vine winding up each post, thorns along its run. Thorns live on the long runs; the centre stays quiet.
8. **The bird's-foot foot (WASH).** "Does the best job at being consistent with the theme and fitting the look of its other design elements." A crane's foot built from the same bone members as the legs: a long front toe and a shorter back toe lying on the floor with bearing pads under their ends, a small inner toe, and on each toe a claw needle whose root is as thick as the toe, curling up and back over the toe rather than out into the room. The leg's tendon runs down into it. It works because the foot is the leg continuing, so the piece reads as one creature.

## Flagged
- **"Wouldn't work as an actual sawhorse."** Horns, needles, stones and chains at the leg heads stood above the work surface with the seat off. Rule: `furniture-scale.md` item 5.
- **"These motifs seem unrealised."** Tiny moths parked in a row under the rail read as half-built blobs. A motif must read at the piece's viewing distance: scale it up into a real member or drop it. Never use repeats as filler.
- **"Not a fan" (all-liquid version).** All liquid ribbon with no carved counterweight and no clear motif reads as plumbing. Use liquid chrome as the accent, not the whole piece.
- **"Far too literal."** A flat clip-art butterfly cut from plate. The motif is built from structure (veins, feathers, wire), may be partial or abstracted, and is never a stencil. Same for jewelry.
- **"Would these wires be purchased?" / "This spike seems fragile."** Name bought hardware on the sheet (`furniture-scale.md` item 10); give exposed spikes thick roots and blunt tips (item 11).
- **"Kinda busy."** Loops, stones, thorns and body overlapping at one centre. One focal point per piece with quiet space around it; where members cross, let one pass clean.
- **"These are ugly" (comb of barbs on the feet).** Spikes in a straight even row read as a comb or saw blade. Spikes wind around a member, or the surface stays smooth.
- **"Why do they all have elf shoes."** One stock foot on every variant: a tapering toe sweeping along the floor and flipping up into a curled tip. A foot is the leg continuing in the same members and material language (see loved item 8), and variants don't share one stock foot.
- **"The literal bug sucks."** A flat butterfly with cut-out wing holes and curled antennae pinned at the crossing of four rods. Second strike for literal insects. A hub where members meet is a structural knot the rods grow out of, never a pictogram.
- **"This handle is awful."** A tidy, generic ornamental handle on a dark moth-themed case. Hardware for an existing piece comes out of that piece's own motifs, and for a dark piece it goes scarier and more gothic. List the motifs already on the piece before drawing hardware; ornament that could go on any box is rejected.
- **"Make sure the tools are based on the old ones ... improvements in the geometry and increasing the complexity of the tooling and reflecting reality more."** When the client likes an existing set, the next round is a refinement, not a redesign. Keep the silhouettes, proportions and materials, and add real-instrument detail: raked and set teeth, a folded back, bevels, needle eyes, lead screws, back springs, pivot pins and graduations. Then work in the host piece's motifs as chasing, finials and guards.

## 9. Cross-stitched leather cord: the motif for every leather grip
The client, 18 Nov 2026, with two shop photos (`img/leather_cord_xstitch_closeup.jpg`, `img/leather_cord_xstitch_loop.jpg`):
"recursively train ur handle design for any leather section to this motif ... like on the saw handle".
What the photos show:
- Round leather cord in natural, vegetable-tanned tan (about 6 to 8 mm across), smooth and slightly glossy, with hand-dyed unevenness.
- Each cord is closed by one seam running along it. The seam is a cross stitch (baseball X) in pale waxed linen thread, the X's sit
  slightly proud with the darker cut leather edge showing between them. The seam spirals around the cord as the cord twists.
- Several cords (four to six) are laid together and twisted into a loose rope, so seams appear and disappear as each cord turns.
  Some cords show the stitched side, some the plain side. The X's are hand-spaced, not machine-even.
Rule: any leather section on a handle, grip, wrap, strap or tether (tools, saw handles, hardware, seats' tethers) uses this:
round stitched leather cords with a pale X-stitched seam, twisted together, instead of a flat wrap or a braided silk cord.
Colour: the client's later pick (19 Nov) is red leather on the gothic tools, a deep oxblood red rather than the tan shown
("perfect just make the leather red"); black where the palette calls for it. The thread stays pale.
Model it as cords (tubes with a shallow seam groove) twisted round the grip core, with the X stitch as thin raised thread
crossing the seam at slightly uneven spacing. Keep the X's hand-spaced (style rule 8: no machine-even rows).
Later notes (19 Nov), with the same motif: "dont clip them", "always run a clipping check for everything always man".
The X stitches must lie on the cords, never sink into them; cord ends sit in hollow collars. See `buildable.md`, check 5.
Also: a spike on a steel rod is one piece of metal, sharp, flowing smoothly out of the rod ("make this spike sharp and
smoothely go into the rest of the piece as its one piece of metal"); saw teeth carry no line through them; ornaments on a
neck plate are never covered by the neck ("so the stained window motif isnt cut off").

## 10. Pierced Gothic tracery signet: a liked reference (24 Nov 2026)
The client sent an Instagram story photo (`img/tracery_signet_aercrawler.jpg`; the story links aercrawler.com, credited as
the likely maker, AERCRAWLER) with "add this to jewlerry skill and style ref". Reference, not to copy.
What it shows:
- A chunky oval signet in silver. The face is pierced right through with Gothic tracery: quatrefoils and trefoil cusps
  locked into a cross-shaped mullion grid, so the finger and shadow show through the holes. A plain raised bezel rim
  frames the face.
- The shoulders are pierced too, with smaller trefoil and cusp openings, so the ornament runs down the shank instead of
  stopping at the head.
- Finish: heavily oxidised recesses (near black) against polished high points on the tracery ribs and rim. The contrast
  draws the pattern; the silver reads old and worn, not bright and new.
- Worn with wide brushed or hairline-textured silver bands (one a thumb ring), plain and soft-edged, so the signet is the
  only ornate piece on the hand. One band carries a small cast flower or shell charm at its edge.
What to take from it:
- Pierce through, don't just carve: real voids give depth, shadow and lightness to a heavy signet. Keep every rib at the
  casting minimum (`casting-rules.md`) and the face genus checked (`insert-layer.md`, `finish.py`).
- Mass first: a big, confident signet head with ornament inside a clean rim, not ornament spreading past its edges.
- Ornament carries onto the shoulders at a smaller scale, so head and shank read as one piece.
- Oxidised ground plus polished highs as the default finish for pierced work (see `render-fidelity.md`).
- Pair ornate pieces with plain textured bands in a set: one loud piece, quiet companions.
Watch: style rule against gothic cross, dagger and fleur-de-lis terminals still holds. Take the pierced tracery and
the finish, not cross or fleur shapes on the shoulders. The design-taste ban on black gothic arches applies to products
and furniture; in jewelry, pierced tracery is liked.
